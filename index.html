Four things to try

01 · the ellipse is Σ's eigenvectors

Contours are level sets of the Mahalanobis distance Δ² = (y−μ)ᵀΣ⁻¹(y−μ) (eq. 3.20). Rewrite it in the rotated frame z = U(y−μ) and it becomes z₁²/λ₁ + z₂²/λ₂ — a plain ellipse. The violet arrows are √λ₁u₁ and √λ₂u₂: eigenvectors set the tilt, eigenvalues set the elongation. and watch the arrows rotate while the ellipse keeps the same area.

02 · marginal = project, conditional = slice

The grey curve on the top edge is the marginal p(y₁) = N(μ₁, σ₁²) — the whole hill flattened onto the y₁ axis, which is why you just read off Σ's diagonal. The amber curve is the conditional p(y₁|y₂): a vertical cut through the hill at the amber line, renormalized. Projecting keeps everything; slicing throws most of it away.

03 · knowing y₂ moves and narrows y₁

E[Y₁|y₂] = μ₁ + ρ(σ₁/σ₂)(y₂−μ₂) slides the amber peak along a line as you drag; V[Y₁|y₂] = σ₁²(1−ρ²) never depends on which y₂ you saw — only on how strong the correlation is. : the amber curve translates but never changes width. That constant width is the whole idea behind missing-value imputation in §3.2.5.

04 · ρ = 0 breaks the link

and the amber curve lands exactly on the grey one, wherever you put the slice. For jointly Gaussian variables, uncorrelated really does mean independent — observing Y₂ tells you nothing. Push ρ toward ±0.95 instead and the conditional collapses to a spike: Y₁ is nearly determined by Y₂.

The density itself

p(y₁,y₂) =
  1 / (2πσ₁σ₂√(1−ρ²))
  × exp{ −1/(2(1−ρ²)) ×
    [ (y₁−μ₁)²/σ₁²
    + (y₂−μ₂)²/σ₂²
    − 2ρ(y₁−μ₁)(y₂−μ₂)/(σ₁σ₂) ] }

Equation 3.18, and it is less intimidating than it looks: the bracket is just the squared Mahalanobis distance written out, the −2ρ(···) cross-term is the only thing a diagonal Σ lacks, and everything in front is the normalizer Z = 2π|Σ|^(1/2) that makes it integrate to 1.
